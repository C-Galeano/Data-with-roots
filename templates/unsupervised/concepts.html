01 · Fundamentals

What is Unsupervised Learning?

Unsupervised learning is a branch of Machine Learning in which the model works with data that has no target variable. There is no column that says what the correct answer is, so the model cannot learn by comparing its predictions with known labels.

Instead, the algorithm looks for structure hidden in the data itself: groups of similar observations, unusual records, or a simpler way to represent many variables. It is useful when labels are expensive or impossible to get, or when the goal is to explore the data and discover patterns that nobody has defined yet.

Main Idea

Learn from the inputs alone and let the data reveal its own organization.

03 · Clustering

What is Clustering?

Clustering is the most common unsupervised task. It divides a dataset into groups called clusters, so that records inside the same cluster are similar to each other and records in different clusters are different.

Similarity is usually measured as a distance between records: two records that are close in the space of the variables are considered similar. The clusters have no names at the start; it is the analyst who interprets them afterwards by looking at their characteristics.

04 · The Algorithm

How Does K-Means Work?

K-Means splits the data into exactly k clusters, where k is chosen by the analyst. Each cluster is represented by one point, its centroid, and the algorithm repeats the same steps until the clusters stop changing.

1
Choose k and initial centroids

Decide how many clusters to look for and place k starting centroids, either by hand, at random, or with a smarter method such as k-means++.

2
Measure distances

Calculate the Euclidean distance from every record to every centroid.

3
Assign each record

Each record joins the cluster of its nearest centroid.

4
Update the centroids

Move each centroid to the average of the records assigned to it, then go back to step 2.

Euclidean distance between a record and a centroid

d(x, C) = √( (x1 − C1)2 + (x2 − C2)2 + … )

It is the straight-line distance between two points. Because it depends on the units of each variable, variables on very different scales should be standardized first.

05 · Centroids and Iterations

The Role of Centroids and Iterations

Centroids

A centroid is the center of a cluster: the average of the coordinates of all the records that belong to it. It does not need to be a real record.

Centroids have two jobs. They decide which records belong to each cluster (every record goes to its nearest centroid), and at the end they summarize each cluster: reading a centroid's coordinates tells you what a typical member of that group looks like.

Iterations

One iteration is a full round of the loop: measure distances, assign records and move the centroids. After each move, some records may be closer to a different centroid, so they change cluster in the next round.

The algorithm converges when no record changes cluster, so the centroids stop moving. The first iterations usually produce the biggest changes; later ones only make small corrections.

06 · Evaluation

How Do We Know the Clusters Are Good?

Without real labels, clustering is evaluated by looking at the shape of the groups: members of a cluster should be close together, and different clusters should be far apart.

WCSS (within-cluster variance)

The sum of the squared distances from every record to its centroid. K-Means tries to make it as small as possible, and it never increases from one iteration to the next. Lower means more compact clusters.

Elbow method

WCSS always falls when k grows, so it cannot choose k by itself. Plotting WCSS for several values of k shows a point, the elbow, after which adding more clusters barely helps. That point is a good k.

Silhouette score

Compares, for each record, how close it is to its own cluster versus the nearest other cluster. It ranges from −1 to 1: values near 1 mean well-separated clusters, near 0 overlapping ones, and negative values suggest misassigned records.

07 · Strengths and Limitations

When to Use K-Means

Strengths
  • Simple to understand and to explain to non-technical people.
  • Fast, even with thousands of records.
  • Centroids give a clear summary of each group.
  • Works well when the groups are compact and roughly round.
Limitations
  • The number of clusters k must be chosen in advance.
  • Different initial centroids can lead to different results.
  • Sensitive to outliers and to the scale of the variables.
  • Struggles with elongated or irregular cluster shapes.
